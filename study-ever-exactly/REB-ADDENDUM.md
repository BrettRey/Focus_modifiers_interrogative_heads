## Study summary for the REB

**Title:** Judgments of English Sentences

**Researcher:** Brett Reynolds, Humber Polytechnic and University of Toronto

**Purpose.** English speakers accept *exactly* after some *wh*-words that
introduce a clause and less readily after others. Corpus evidence and the
researcher's own judgments suggest that *exactly* costs little after
*whatever* (*The committee rejected whatever exactly the consultant had
proposed*) and more after plain *what* (*The committee rejected what
exactly the consultant had proposed*). This study estimates that
difference from ratings by native speakers.

**Procedure.** An online survey of about 15 minutes (to be confirmed in
the pilot). After a consent screen and three practice trials,
participants rate 50 English sentences, one at a time, on a 1-7 scale for
how natural each sounds. A second block shows 16 of the sentences again
and asks two multiple-choice questions about each (whether it is a
statement or a question; whether the speaker seems to know which thing is
meant). Background and short post-task questions follow, then a debrief
screen.

**Materials.** 27 sentence frames (each participant sees 24), each in four versions (*whatever* or
*what*, with or without *exactly*); each participant sees every frame
once, six in each version, in a separately randomized order. 24 filler
sentences (12 natural, 12 clearly unnatural) and 2 attention checks
("To show you're reading carefully, please choose 2 for this sentence";
the second asks for 6). Full items and the per-participant decks:
`protocol.md`, `items/all_items.csv`, `items/decks/`. Sentences were
drafted with AI assistance and checked by the researcher; five frames
are adapted from sentences found on public websites.

**Possible second set of frames.** If the pilot shows that frames differ
substantially in how much *exactly* lowers the ratings, a second set of
24 frames of the same kind will be written (ordinary statements on
everyday topics with *whatever* or *what*, with or without *exactly*), and
the 48 frames will be split across raters, each rater still seeing 24.
The task, the number of trials, the duration and the risk stay the same.
The new sentences go to the REB as a change request before they're used.

**Participants.** Adults who report being native speakers of English,
recruited through the platform. A pilot of about 30 (at most 40
recruited) checks timing, the questions and the materials. The main study
then aims for 100 after exclusions, 150 if the budget allows (at most 180
recruited). The main-study size comes from a design analysis (Gelman &
Carlin 2014): with 100 raters the simulated 95% interval for the key
effect is about ±0.3 points on the 7-point scale. That figure rests on
variance assumptions borrowed from a related study, to be replaced by
pilot estimates; `protocol.md` states them. Pilot participants don't
take part in the main study.

**Exclusions from analysis.** Participants who fail either attention
check; participants whose mean rating of the unnatural fillers is not at
least one point below their mean rating of the natural fillers;
incomplete sessions. Exclusion from analysis doesn't affect payment
beyond what the platform's own terms specify.

**Risks.** Minimal. Some sentences may sound unusual or ungrammatical,
and the task may feel repetitive. No sensitive topics.

**Disclosure.** Participants are told the study is about how people judge
English sentences. The specific contrast being tested isn't described
beforehand, because knowing it could change the ratings. There is no
deception, and the debrief screen explains the purpose.

**Benefits.** No direct benefit to participants. The results bear on how
English grammar restricts modifiers such as *exactly*.

**Compensation.** [Platform-specific amount and terms, as for the *whose*
study.]

**Researcher's own judgments.** The researcher's judgments motivated the
study and will be reported separately as such. They aren't participant
data and aren't pooled with participant ratings.

## Participant information and consent

**Study title:** Judgments of English Sentences

**Researcher:** Brett Reynolds, Humber Polytechnic and University of Toronto  
**Contact:** brett.reynolds@humber.ca

### Invitation

You are invited to take part in a research study about how English
speakers judge sentences. You are being invited because you report that
you are an adult native speaker of English.

### What you will do

You will complete an online survey of about 15 minutes. First you will
rate 50 English sentences, one at a time, on a 1-7 scale for how natural
each sounds. Then you will see 16 of the sentences again and answer two
short multiple-choice questions about each. At the end we ask a few
background questions (age range, native language and other languages,
the region whose English you speak, education, and whether you have
studied linguistics) and three short questions about the task (whether
you noticed any patterns, how you decided on your ratings, and whether
anything went wrong technically). You may answer the last three in your
own words or leave them blank.

### Eligibility

You may participate if you:

- are 18 years of age or older;
- are a native speaker of English;
- can complete an online reading task in English.

Please do not participate if you have already completed this study or
its pilot.

## Data-collection tool

- consent screen;
- 3 practice trials (natural, in between, unnatural), not analysed;
- 50 rating trials: 24 targets, 24 fillers, 2 attention checks, in the
  participant's own pre-generated random order (`items/decks/P###.csv`);
- 7-point scale, 1 = very unnatural, 7 = very natural; minimum 2-second
  display before response, if supported;
- interpretation block: 16 sentences, each with "Is this sentence a
  statement or a question?" (A statement / A question / Can't tell) and
  "Does the speaker seem to know which [noun] it was?" (*they were* for
  plural nouns such as *items*) (Yes, they know
  which / No, they don't know which / Can't tell), option order as in the
  deck;
- background questions (age range, native language, other languages,
  dialect region, education, linguistics training);
- post-task questions (noticed patterns, rating strategy, technical
  problems), optional free text;
- debrief screen.

### Participant-facing wording (new 2026-10-28, not yet reviewed by Brett)

**Instructions screen.** In this study you'll read English sentences one
at a time and rate how natural each one sounds to you, from 1 (very
unnatural) to 7 (very natural). There are no right or wrong answers.
We're interested in how the sentence sounds to you, not in rules you may
have learned at school. Some sentences are meant to sound fine and some
aren't. Please read each one carefully and go with your first
impression. You'll start with three practice sentences.

**Rating question** (every rating trial): "How natural is this sentence?
1 = very unnatural, 7 = very natural".

**Interpretation block, opening screen.** Now you'll see some of the
sentences again. For each one, please answer two short questions about
how you understand it.

**Background questions** (each with "Prefer not to say"):

1. What is your age? 18-24 / 25-34 / 35-44 / 45-54 / 55-64 / 65 or older
2. Which language or languages did you learn from birth or early
   childhood? English only / English and at least one other language /
   Another language or languages, not English
3. Do you speak any other language fluently? Yes / No
4. Where did you mainly grow up? Canada / United States / United Kingdom
   or Ireland / Australia or New Zealand / Another country
5. What is the highest level of education you've completed? Secondary
   school or less / Some college or university / College diploma or
   bachelor's degree / Graduate or professional degree
6. Have you ever taken a course in linguistics or English grammar at a
   college or university? Yes / No / Not sure

**Post-task questions** (optional, free text):

7. Did you notice any patterns in the sentences? If so, what?
8. How did you decide on your ratings?
9. Did anything go wrong technically (for example, a page that didn't
   load)?
